Every client meeting starts the same way now. Somewhere between the brief and the budget, someone asks about AI. Usually it is phrased as a question about speed – can it get done faster now, can it get done cheaper. Sometimes it is phrased as a worry – is a designer still needed at all.

I have been using AI tools inside my actual production work for a while now, across branding, 3D, motion and UI. Not as a demo, as part of shipping real projects. So instead of another hype post or another doom post, here is where AI in design workflow genuinely earns its place for me, and where it flatly does not.

Where AI in a design workflow actually saves time

The honest wins are in the unglamorous middle of a project, not the parts anyone puts in a portfolio.

Reference gathering is the clearest one. Early on a project I used to spend an hour building a moodboard from scattered Pinterest searches and half-remembered Dribbble shots. Now I can describe a direction in words and get a wide spread of visual starting points back in minutes, then pick what is actually useful and throw the rest away. It has not changed what I am looking for, it has changed how fast I get to look at options.

Rough exploration is the second one. When I am still deciding between three or four directions for a mark or a layout, generating quick visual variations lets me see more bad ideas faster, which is honestly how you get to the good idea faster too. This is a sketchbook, not a final render.

Then there is the pure production grunt work: upscaling a low-res asset a client sends over, cleaning up backgrounds, batch-resizing crops for a dozen ad formats, quick retouching passes. This is the AI use case with zero downside. Nobody’s craft was ever proven in a background eraser.

Where it does not replace craft

This is the part that gets left out of most AI-in-design content, because it is less exciting to write about.

Concept development does not come from a prompt. It comes from understanding a business that a client has not fully articulated even to themselves yet, and translating that into a visual idea nobody has quite made before. AI tools can show you what already exists, remixed. They cannot tell you what a specific brand needs to say that no other brand in its category is saying. That is still a human read on a human problem.

Brand strategy is the same story at a higher level. A logo or identity system is not just an image, it is a decision about how a company wants to be perceived for years, defended across a hundred future applications nobody has designed yet. Something like Cerebro, where the mark had to fuse the idea of a human brain with a digital marketing agency’s whole visual language, was not a generation problem. It was a judgment problem: what single idea earns the right to represent this business, and will it still hold up on a business card, a favicon and a billboard at once. That call is still entirely mine to make.

Final 3D and motion polish is another wall AI has not gotten past, in my experience. On a piece like the Motorola Transistor Concept, the work that actually made it feel real was in materials, lighting and render passes tuned by hand until the object felt physically present instead of merely plausible. That last ten percent, the part where something goes from looking generated to looking made, is still craft, iteration and a trained eye for when it is actually done.

And then there is client taste, which no tool manages for you. Reading a vague piece of feedback correctly, knowing which round of revisions is really about the logo and which is really about something the client has not said out loud yet, that relationship is still fully human on both ends.

What this actually changes about the job

The realistic shift is not fewer designers, it is a different ratio of hours. Less time on the repetitive setup work, more time on the decisions that were always the actual job: the concept, the strategy, the final polish, the client conversation. If anything, cutting the grunt work down means there is more room to spend on the parts of a project that build genuine craft, the same way better tooling changed motion work without replacing the eye behind it – something I got into more in Why Brands Need a Motion Layer Now, Not Just a Logo.

So if you are a client wondering whether AI means a project should cost less or move faster: sometimes, on the parts that were always overhead. If you are wondering whether it replaces the judgment you are actually paying for, no. That part still has to be earned the old way.
